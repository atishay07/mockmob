# RESEARCH-CHARACTERS: what Gen Z likes in characters, and what MockMob's character world should be

Research pass, 19 Oct 2026, for the Mob v3 work. Written for the main session and the design agents.
Method: web search and page fetches on 19 Oct 2026. The search tool is US-centred, so India-specific data is thin. Where a claim is a secondary source, a single source or my own judgement, it is marked. Nothing here is a MockMob fact; the Mob facts live in `MOB-FACTS.md`.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 Genmoji and the soft glossy 3D emoji look

- What it is: Apple's on-device generator for custom emoji-style stickers from a text description, launched to the public with iOS 18.2 in December 2024. It can be used inline, as a large sticker or as a Tapback. Apple blocks violent, nude, political and copyrighted requests. (MacRumors Genmoji guide, 1 Nov 2024.)
- What the form gets right: it reads like emoji (small, round, high contrast, one clear emotion), so it survives at chat-bubble size and drops into a sentence. The appeal is not the glossy render; it is that you can say a thing about yourself or a friend that no standard emoji says.
- Reception is mixed, which matters for us: reporting in 2026 says results often do not match the prompt or Apple's own adverts, the models are heavy on battery, and the feature is hidden away so it is rarely used. Apple is reportedly moving it into the keyboard in iOS 27. (MacTrast, May 2026, relaying Bloomberg's Gurman; secondary.) Lesson: a generator is a novelty; a fixed, well-drawn cast people can send daily is what gets used.
- Gen Z in 2026 is leaning away from slick. BUCK's 2026 predictions (12 Jan 2026) say brands are moving back to mascots as emotional anchors, that work that looks plainly human-made is returning (hand-drawn frames, stop-motion, imperfect materials), and that brands will let users remix their characters. One contributor puts it as imperfection being the signal. These are industry opinions, not measurements.
- Surveys on AI-made content: Goldman Sachs (Aug 2025, via EMARKETER) found 54% of consumers prefer no AI involvement in creative work, against 13% for shopping; Gartner (published Mar 2026, secondary write-up) found half of US consumers prefer brands that avoid generative AI in consumer-facing content. The pattern across sources is that AI-assisted is tolerated and AI-replaced is not. These are consumer figures, not Gen Z-only.
- Implication for the owner's wish to use an image generator: fine as a tool for exploration and finishing, but the cast must be art-directed, consistent, hand-corrected and carry visible craft (a fold, a crease, a pencil mark). If it looks like a generator output it will read as slop. How not to copy Apple: no photoreal-adjacent glossy faces, no yellow emoji blob, no "describe an emoji" prompt box (also a safety risk for minors). Apple's look is a rendered gloss on a round yellow face; ours is paper, ink, lamp light and Mobi's visor.

### 1.2 Collectible toy culture (Pop Mart, Labubu, Sonny Angel, Smiski, blind boxes)

- Scale and recency: Pop Mart reported 2025 revenue of about 37.1 billion yuan (about USD 5.4 billion), up about 185%, with Labubu and The Monsters franchise at about 38% of revenue; shares then fell on concentration worries and analysts doubt another Labubu is ready. (Jing Daily, 25 Mar 2026; China Daily, 9 Apr 2026; KrASIA, 2026.) Earlier: 2024 revenue USD 1.8 billion, Labubu 2024 revenue USD 400 million, up 726%. (Asia Matters for America, 21 May 2025.) Lesson: the craze is real but single-character dependence is a business risk; a world with many characters is sturdier.
- Why people love them, with the strength of evidence:
  - Surprise and the chase: blind boxes hide which figure you get, and each series has a secret figure, often quoted at about 1 in 144. (Asia Matters for America, 21 May 2025; Varsity-type student pieces; secondary.) One study quoted in a student article says about 70% of buyers keep buying to finish a set (secondary, I could not open the original).
  - Ugly-cute and imperfection: Labubu has fangs and a mischievous grin, unlike Hello Kitty; the Istituto Marangoni piece (28 May 2025) says Gen Z does not want polished but weird, niche and full of vibes. Sanrio's Kuromi is described the same way as a character that embraces flaws (editorial, not a survey).
  - Lore: Labubu comes from the illustrator Kasing Lung's fairy-tale world, introduced in 2015, with a cast of other odd characters; Sonny Angel was framed as comfort for stressful adult life; Smiski are framed as little guardian fairies. (Asia Matters for America, 21 May 2025; Istituto Marangoni, 28 May 2025.)
  - Emotion as identity: Pop Mart's Crybaby and Hirono sell permission to feel something (it is okay to cry; life's highs and lows). Chiikawa works on small struggles and simple joys. (Lifestyle and blog pieces, 2025-2026; editorial, not research.) Lesson for an exam app: characters that are anxious, tired and still turn up are relatable to a 16-year-old before a board exam.
  - Display and dressing up: bag charms, phone cases, shelves, fit checks with tiny outfits, miniature dioramas. #Labubu had over 1.4 million TikTok posts at the time of that article (Istituto Marangoni, 28 May 2025). Smiski's glow-in-the-dark finish is a small, ownable gimmick; about 20% of Smiski products use glow powder (Asia Matters for America).
  - Cheap, frequent, social: low price per box, unboxing videos and community status do the marketing.
- India: Labubu entered through scarcity and community, not mass advertising: AnimeCons, Comic Cons, art toy fairs, drops announced about 24 hours ahead on Telegram and Instagram, online lotteries for rare editions (marketing newsletter, 20 Aug 2025; opinion-style, no sales figures). I found no India teen sales or survey figures for 2025-2026. Treat "Indian teens love blind boxes" as likely but unproven; the global pattern is the safer evidence.
- Regulator note: China already bans blind-box sales to under-8s, and a People's Daily commentary in June 2025 urged tighter rules because of addictive buying by minors (Malay Mail, 20 Jun 2025; secondary). The gambling comparison follows the random, paid mechanic, which we do not use.

### 1.3 Mascots that won Gen Z on social media

- Duolingo's Duo: organic start (users joking about passive-aggressive reminders), then the brand leaned in with a team that ships a video in about 30 minutes, treats Duo as a character with a life (a restraining order, an obsession with Dua Lipa, a red-carpet appearance) and replies to comments in character. Nearly 8 million TikTok followers by Sept 2023, and the marketing director said a large share of user growth comes from TikTok. Separate country accounts (India included) localise the voice. (Fast Company Middle East, 10 Sept 2023.)
- The cast is part of the trick: Lily is the apathetic purple teenager, Zari is the high-energy foil; Duolingo planned a 90s-sitcom style series for Lily on social. (Fast Company, 2023.) Cast members give different people someone to be, and give a feed more than one joke.
- Stunts: Duo's "death" in Feb 2025, ended with fans earning XP to bring him back (goal of 50 billion XP). Meltwater counted about 169,000 mentions in two weeks; larger impression figures vary by source and are unreliable. NPR (26 Feb 2025) reported mixed reaction to the reveal. (NoGood, 10 Mar 2025; Meltwater; NPR.) Lesson: the audience takes part in the story through the product's own currency, but the stunt only works because the character already had years of lore.
- Duo's menace is a joke about the streak. That is a risk for us, not a template: the research on streaks is mostly blogs, but a small student thesis reported some pressure and anxiety from streaks and rankings, and critics call the guilt loop manipulative (secondary, thin evidence). Our rule of no guilt or loss threats stands.
- Caution from the same period: comparisons to "unhinged" marketing are now everywhere (NoGood lists several imitators), and a campus piece (19 Mar 2025) notes tastes move fast. Copying a tone is the quickest way to look dated.

### 1.4 Avatar and collection systems teens use

- Snapchat Bitmoji: Snap reports 2.7 billion-plus avatars created and about 320 million daily users interacting with them; the standalone app ends 30 June 2026. (Snap statements via trade press, late 2025 to 2026.) In Nov 2025 Snap brought back a classic 2D "Comic Bitmoji" after a petition of nearly 100,000 signatures, but made it a Snapchat+ feature (Fast Company; Social Media Today; Nov 2025). Snap's 2025 Recap says the 6/7 Bitmoji sticker became the most-used sticker since launch (B&T, 19 Dec 2025). Lessons: people care a lot about the style of their avatar; a slang meme travelled through a sticker; the look users demanded back was the simple, drawn one.
- Zepeto: Naver's 3D avatar world with a claimed 340 million users by 2022 (Financial Times via a trade summary), heavily female and teen; parent guides flag stranger-contact risks (Internet Matters; school notice 22 Apr 2025). Lesson: the avatar was the draw; the open social layer was the risk. We keep the avatar and drop the stranger layer.
- Finch: a virtual bird you care for by completing self-care tasks; rewards are stones spent on clothes and furniture; premium adds customisation; widget keeps the bird on the home screen. Reviews praise the cute design and rewards loop. (Internet Matters; Kimola; reviews; undated.) Lesson: care for a character plus cosmetic spending of earned currency is a proven teen-friendly loop; the pet reflects your effort, it does not punish.
- Duolingo: the cast, streak and the Duo costume on the merch store are the visible identity; fan content (streak parties, costumes) extends reach (Fast Company, 2023).
- Pokemon Sleep: a collection of looks, not just species. The Sleep Style Dex tracks different sleeping poses per Pokemon, some tied to specific behaviours, with dex goals giving rewards (Bulbapedia; Sportskeeda; undated). Lesson: collecting variants of one character (poses) is richer than collecting many characters, and the progress bar is the motivator.
- Indian apps: I found no good public data on which Indian apps teens use for avatars. Known and reported: FamPay (teen fintech, parental consent) lets teens choose card doodles and fonts, has a social feed and gamified savings, with over 2 million registered users within eight months of launch (earlier reporting, undated). Meta's India Gen Z trend report (WGSN, Dec 2023) says 90% of Gen Z belong to a fandom and that bad taste in memes is the top turn-off for almost a third of Indian respondents; a Duolingo India agency case study (self-reported) says localised, meme-driven reels reached over 10 million monthly. Gap: I did not verify ShareChat, Moj, Free Fire or BGMI avatar behaviour; the owner's team should check with real 16-17-year-olds.

### 1.5 Ethics, law and platform rules for digital collectibles for minors in India

- India: the Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act, 2025 targets online money games (stakes, convertible tokens or "other stakes", enrichment expectation). Social games without stakes are allowed; commencement and rules were still pending when the sources I read were written. The sources do not say how it treats ordinary cosmetic purchases or loot-box mechanics. (SCC Online, 24 Aug 2025; Mondaq; Nishith Desai; secondary.)
- DPDP Rules 2025: notified 13/14 Nov 2025 with phased dates; the children's rules (verifiable parental consent for under-18s; no tracking or behavioural monitoring and no targeted advertising to children, with narrow exemptions) apply from 14 May 2027. (AZB, 19 Nov 2025; AMS Shardul; Bar and Bench on the draft; secondary.) Not legal advice, but it fits our design: no behavioural profiling of students for collectible offers, no ad targeting.
- Platform rules: Apple (Dec 2017, section 3.1.1) and Google Play (May 2019) require odds disclosure before purchasing randomised virtual items. They govern paid randomness; we have none. (Fenwick; TouchArcade; Pocketgamer.biz; the current text should be re-read at submission.)
- Elsewhere: Brazil's Law 15,211/2025 (signed 17 Sept 2025, in force 2026) treats loot boxes as off-limits to minors (Mattos Filho; Demarest; secondary). Belgium treats them as gambling. UK ad regulators updated loot box disclosure guidance in 2025 (Outlook Respawn). The direction of travel is clear and strict.
- Conclusion: paid or consumable randomness for under-18s is the one thing to rule out. Earned, deterministic, non-transferable, non-convertible cosmetics are the safe zone.

## 2. Ten design principles for MockMob's characters

1. One world, one hand. A single art direction (Mobi's folded paper, visor eyes, lamp light) across all characters, so the cast is recognisably MockMob in a screenshot. Brand first, then personality.
2. Imperfect on purpose. A visible crease, a dog-ear, a pencil smudge, a slightly wonky topper. Gen Z reads polish as corporate and AI-smooth as cheap; craft marks read as a person made it.
3. Ugly-cute, not only cute. Give each character one odd trait (a bent antenna, a coffee ring, a tired eyelid). Odd traits are what get named, memed and defended.
4. Emotion before decoration. Each character has a feeling it stands for (nervous, overconfident, sleepy-but-here, chaotic, careful, showoff). Teens pick the one that feels like them on a bad Tuesday. Expression lives in the eyes and posture, as Mobi's does.
5. Readable at disc size. If it does not read at 40 px as a sticker, a shelf tile and a podium figure, it does not ship. Silhouette and one colour first.
6. Personal, not identity-revealing. Colour, one accessory and a name tag are the self-expression; never a photo, a face scan or a free-text caption.
7. Earned, not bought. Characters, outfits and variants come from real study events. Nothing in the Mob is for sale. The owner's rule on Pro (only Pro members start games) stays a feature rule, not a character-selling rule.
8. A cast, not a mascot plus extras. Duo's reach came with Lily, Zari and the rest. Mobi stays the guide; the Mob's characters are the friends. Different people get different people to be.
9. Light-hearted, never shaming. They can be anxious, sleepy, over-dramatic about a missed question, but never smug about someone's wrong answer, never guilting, never threatening a streak.
10. Made to be shared. Every character has a still sticker, a short loop and a name that can be said aloud. If a friend cannot say its name in a WhatsApp voice note, rename it.

## 3. Collectibility without money

My own design, informed by sections 1.2 and 1.4. No randomness is bought or consumed.

- The shelf. Each student has a shelf (their cabinet or desk) and the Mob has a shared one. Slots are visible from day one: empty ones show a dashed outline and the true reason that earns them. No hidden unlocks, no countdown pressure.
- Earned reveal moments. The Pokemon Sleep and blind-box pull is mostly the reveal. We keep the reveal and lose the gamble: a milestone (first repaired trap, a Mob finishing a paper together, a finished subject chapter) opens a folded paper that unfolds into the character. The student knows what milestone opens it; the surprise is the look and a small quirk, not whether they get anything.
- Rare variants from milestones. Variants (foil edge, glow-in-the-dark ink like Smiski, a lamp-lit version, a pencil-sketch version) come from rarer true events: ten repaired mistakes that stayed fixed, a Mob that sat its paper six weeks in a row. Rare means hard to do, never lucky. Show the rule on the locked variant.
- Variant, not only species. Take the Pokemon Sleep lesson: many poses and looks of the same few characters give more collecting depth than 40 characters, and cost less art. Aim for about 8 to 12 characters, each with 3 to 5 variants.
- Keep the set finite and visible. A 1-in-144 secret figure is the gambling shape. Instead, one clearly shown "last page" variant per character that is earned by effort.
- Never lost. Once earned, always kept, even after leaving a Mob (matches the existing rule that quest characters are kept for good). No decay, no expiry, no loss threats.
- Not tradable, not convertible, not for sale. No trading, no resale, no transfer between accounts. This keeps us far from the "convertible items" idea in the Online Gaming Act and from marketplace risks for minors.
- Show, don't leak. Characters appear only to people in the same Mob; shelf visibility is a per-Mob switch. No leaderboards of who owns what outside the Mob, no "N students got this" counters at launch.
- Dress-up, with earned items only. Outfits, toppers and tiny props (the fit-check behaviour of Labubu fans) come from subjects: a compass for Maths, a quill for English. Keep it to a few slots so every combination looks designed.
- The sticker economy is free. Preset stickers (fixed list, no free text) are the reaction vocabulary and are part of the cast's lines.
- Honesty rule. If any item is ever time-limited, say so up front and never carry "last chance" language. At launch there are none.

## 4. How a story can work

Direction, not final lore. It should sit on what MockMob already is: unlimited mocks from one bank, paper, a lamp, a mistake you fix and prove you fixed.

- The world: a single endless strip of paper, a Mobius strip with one side, the shape of "unlimited mocks from one bank". Every mock paper is cut from it. The family lives in the fold. (This ties to the owner's Mobius paper family liking and to the "mo" icon.)
- Why the characters exist: every mistake leaves a crease in the paper. Left alone, creases harden into traps. When someone fixes one and it holds, the crease smooths and a small folded creature unfolds from it. So each character is a fixed mistake with a face. This turns the core promise (find where marks slip, fix it, check the fix holds) into the origin myth, and keeps the story true to the product.
- The cast, by feeling (examples, names to be tested aloud): the one who rushes, the one who second-guesses, the one who skips the hard questions, the one who learns at midnight, the one who always guesses well, the one who panics at the clock. Each has a weakness the exam really exposes and a small way to beat it. Mobi is the guide who knows the strip.
- Ties to the exam: character quirks map to real CUET habits (the 72-second pace, negative marking, section choice, last-ten-minutes). The story gives students a friendly name for their own habits, which is also a stronger result screen than a number.
- A reason to come back, without guilt: new variants come from effort, not from a calendar. There is no villain called "forgetting".
- Cross-language: a Hinglish name pool and a line each in plain English; keep jokes plain and warm, in the owner's voice, no telegram style.
- Cautions: do not lean on invented slang ("aura", "rizz") the owner finds cringe (noted in COMMUNITY-1018). Do not add lore for lore's sake: every character must map to a real product moment.

## 5. Social media uses

- Reels and Shorts: 8 to 15 second loops of a character doing its habit (the rusher answers in 3 seconds and regrets it), on the pace of the real exam. Cut from the same animations used in the app, so marketing costs nothing extra.
- Character-per-habit memes: "which exam habit are you" with the cast; students send it to the friend who is that character. This travels in WhatsApp as a sticker pack, which suits India (stickers and memes are how Indian Gen Z connects, per Meta's 2023 trend data).
- The unfold reveal as a format: a 6-second clip of a paper unfolding into a character is a natural unboxing video, with none of the paid-box problem. Students can film their own shelf.
- Fit checks and shelf tours: allow screenshot-friendly shelf and outfit views (no names, no photos, first name optional), mirroring the Labubu bag charm and Smiski shelf behaviour.
- Printables: a free, printable character sheet and a folding template (paper craft) for school desks and notebook covers. It fits the paper world and gives teachers and parents something harmless to share.
- The brand voice: a light, consistent character voice (dry, kind, slightly dramatic) like Duo's, but not menacing. Reply in character, ship quickly, keep a visible human maker. A named illustrator's hand-drawn process posts fit BUCK's "imperfection is the signal".
- Truthful by default: campaigns show real features and real data (for example "the Mob finished its paper", "a mistake fixed and held"), not invented activity.
- Later, not at launch: seasonal packs tied to exam calendar moments (board results, CUET date), collabs with stationery brands. The People-in-the-Mob rule holds: no minors' faces or names in marketing.

## 6. What to avoid

- Paid randomness of any kind: no gacha, no mystery boxes, no paid coins, no "pity" timers. Legal risk (Brazil's 2025 law, platform odds rules, Indian gaming law uncertainty) and ethical risk with 16-17-year-olds.
- Variable-reward slot behaviour even when free: no "spin" that wastes effort, no daily wheel, no near-miss animations.
- Streak guilt: no sad characters when someone misses, no "your streak is dying", no loss threats, no naming who has not played.
- Copying Apple's Genmoji look or yellow emoji blob; copying Duolingo's menace tone; copying the RPM-style avatar app the owner finds cringe.
- Photoreal, glossy, over-smooth AI faces. If an image generator is used, hand-finish and keep the paper craft marks. Do not announce "AI-made" as a feature; do not hide it either if asked.
- Free text between users, user-generated character captions, photo upload or face scanning.
- Cross-Mob rankings, crowds, "students online", fake opponents. Pace Mobi stays plainly Mobi.
- Characters that need lore to be understood on first sight. Each one must read in 2 seconds on a podium or a sticker.
- Too many characters: 24 weak silhouettes lose to 10 strong ones (the Den v2 notes already rate Crane, Phirki and Pankha as weakest).
- Social-media stunts that depend on a big audience, like Duo's "death", before a cast and lore exist. They need years of groundwork.
- Gendered or caste/region-coded jokes; characters that stand for a region, religion or caste. Check names with a diverse review group.

## 7. Evidence gaps to close

- India teen preferences: no reliable public data found on which characters, sticker packs or avatar apps 16-17-year-olds in India use. Run five short interviews or a poll of real students before locking the cast.
- Streak ethics for minors: thin evidence; rely on the owner's no-guilt rule.
- Legal: take a quick opinion from counsel on cosmetic rewards under the Online Gaming Act and the DPDP children's rules (from May 2027) and re-read current Apple and Google policy text at submission.
- Pop Mart and Duolingo figures here are mostly secondary or trade-press numbers; do not quote them in public copy.
